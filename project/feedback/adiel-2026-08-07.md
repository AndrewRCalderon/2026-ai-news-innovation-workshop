## Notes by page

### Setup guide (`/setup.html`)

1. **Make the install steps a checklist?** Checkboxes will help students can
   see they're making progress as they go through each step. Then there
   could be a submit button at the end (or a google form) so they can tell
   us they've done it and any issues they run into.

   Ask them to go through the steps and hit submit before Day 1. That way we
   know in advance whether everybody got through it, and we can see the
   challenges they ran into with the installs before class starts — so we
   know going in if there's a bunch of issues we're going to need to deal
   with.

2. **Add screenshots.** For things like opening the Claude Code extension in
   VS Code — little icons and images they can follow along with. For the
   setup guide, we want as many things to help them as possible.

3. **Keep the terminal optional and Git light.** Unless we know some of them
   are technical, we probably don't want to lead with the
   install-from-your-terminal instructions — make it an optional dropdown:
   "if you're already comfortable with the terminal, this is even simpler if
   you do it that way." Let's not assume anybody is comfortable with the
   terminal, and I don't want them to feel like they have to learn much
   about Git. This fits with my larger question about how much git and
   github to have them deal with.

### Day 1 — Industry Conversation

8. **Rethink how slide 1 is framed.** I like all the things that are in that
   first slide — where the dichotomies and the debates are. But I wonder if
   we can introduce it differently or one at a time, or frame it a little
   differently? If I were a student, I don't think I'd know what to do with
   seeing all of that on the board.

   So I wonder if what we're actually after is getting them to generate
   ideas about where some of the disagreements are, and then putting your
   list up on the board?

   My concern is that how it's framed right now is almost a leading question
   — progress versus caution, accessibility versus concentration. It invites
   absolutism to one side, and they're generally value-driven students, so I
   feel like they'll get locked in a little. Maybe I'm overanalyzing this,
   but I want them to think about it before we present dichotomies to them.
   That's my initial reaction — and maybe you're already planning this and I
   just can't see it from the slides.

9. **Reframe the one-sentence labor point to hit it harder.** This feels

   the biggest first disruptions in coding, and that it likely applies to
   journalism and most other jobs the same way: it's not going to outright
   replace people, it's changing what work humans do.

10. **Slides 1–3 didn't quite make sense in order to me.** Slide 3 feels a

11. **One common use case I didn't see.** Probably the most common thing is

### Day 1 — Use Cases

**I like these use cases.** Notes below.

12. **Two examples that have aged out.** Probably we avoid any from 2023 or
    earlier — in particular the lawyer who filed the legal brief with
    made-up cases; the legal AI services have moved forward immensely since
    then. And the UPS ORION example is from 2021. Could be much better to
    introduce them to where legal AI actually is now. Ex: Harvey AI is one
    of the big ones. It might be useful to note that high-stakes legal
    discovery is using AI to help parse through massive document dumps.
    There's actually a lot of overlap between how we do our reporting and
    how lawyers do some of theirs — just to a slightly different end. I also
    have a friend who was just hired to AI finance work, so it could be
    useful to note all these places where accuracy matters that are going
    all in on AI stuff and where they see use cases.

13. **Some of these examples overlap with journalism more than the label
    suggests.** A lot are filed as use cases outside of journalism, but I'd
    argue they actually have a Venn diagram with it. Or they're things used
    on the business side of journalism that came from elsewhere, like the
    customer service and comment moderation ones.

14. **The "can't do" claims are more absolute than we probably want.** "It
    can't do anything in real time" feels misleading — it can, if you make a
    workflow for it. The training data cuts off and they need to be mindful
    of that but they can direct it to take in and make use of new
    information, even realtime updates. Same with "it can't do investigative
    journalism" — it can't do certain *pieces* of it, but there's a lot it
    can help with that's part of that process.

15. **Add patterns reporters actually recognize to the works-when column.**
    It's useful for finding needles in haystacks, pattern recognition, and
    lead generation, etc.

    "Speed matters more than perfection" is fair, but it might also help to
    frame it as useful when you're sifting for at-least numbers. We don't
    need the absolute total of every law proposed in every state related to
    book banning — we want to find as many examples of it as we can. I think
    that might be a useful framing.

16. **I really like the practical question part.** I wonder if this one
    should be reframed:

    > Is the task repetitive and well-structured, or does it need original
    > judgment?

    Isn't the deeper question: does it need original judgment *every single
    time*, or can I make the judgment calls when I design the process so
    that it's surfacing a smaller number that I have to make realtime
    judgment calls on?
