## Things to maybe add?

### 1. Glossary

I don't think we have a place where we specifically explain APIs, CLIs and MCPs,
along with model and effort selection for different tasks, and then they have to
learn about commits and pushes and forking repos, and what tools to choose for
deploying (vercel, supabase, turso, chainlit, etc...).

I wonder if it is part of the course where we have them collectively try to make
their Claude preferences help them learn about new tools and find source
documentation to help them make informed choices, then are working together to
build a class glossary (where they're all teaching claude to PR the same
glossary doc) and then we just clean it up a bit at the end for them? Might be
too complicated but it seems like a useful thing to teach them, especially since
it will be a lot of terms very quickly, which could be overwhelming.

This could be way too big an add, but it also seemed maybe fun for them and like
a good learning exercise because they'll get to see what it looks like when they
just tell claude to send notes on something and how messy that gets.

### 2. Common pitfalls/what to watch out for

It could also be good to have a few slides and/or a guide devoted to things to
watch out for - like accidentally publishing API keys and other credentials,
accidentally using a huge amount of API budget (maybe how to test on a mini
model or with a small batch, etc.) Basically, I'm wondering if there's room for
a "these are mistakes to watch out for when you're new to using AI-assisted
coding" lesson. Maybe there's an exercise they can follow along with where they
have to catch some of these from a repo we give them?

There's a bunch of stuff in this vein I saw in the slides, I just think a
concentrated short lesson could be a big help for them.

The question is then, where it should sit — maybe Day 2 before the build block,
or Day 3 with Safe & Auditable Design?

## Suggested page changes

### 1. Product Design (§02) — audience preferences around AI

I saw you used the Reuters Institute's Oct 2025 Generative AI report for the
framing stat. If you haven't seen them, here are a few more recent ones that I
think could inform slides 2 and 4 of the product design deck.

I think these illustrate how new data about audience preferences and behaviors
around AI is constantly emerging and things are moving fast enough that many
people are going to be at many different levels of comfort and preference, and
what you build for today could be really different audience preferences than
what you'd build for in 6 months. I find the part of the Daudens piece on making
every claim clickable back to its source really interesting — readers scored
that article higher on transparency and claim-data alignment, which I think gets
at what you had on slide 4?

- Reuters Institute, Digital News Report 2026, ["Emerging uses of AI chatbots for news
  and what it means for journalism"](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026/emerging-uses-ai-chatbots-news-and-what-it-means-journalism),
  June 16 2026 — chatbot use for news rose from 7% to 10% globally in a single year
- CNTI, ["Action, Ease & Personalization: AI Chatbot News Experiences"](https://cnti.org/reports/chatbots-for-news/),
  Jan 22 2026 — people use chatbots to *act* on what's happening and to *understand*
  it, far more than to know about it or feel something about it
- Florent Daudens, ["The half of journalism AI can't do yet"](https://fdaudens.substack.com/p/is-ai-better-at-our-jobs-than-us),
  June 23 2026 — in the study he's writing about, 93% of the AI article's claims linked
  back to the code or citation behind them, against 25% for the human-written

### 2. AI-Human Design (§04) — how we're explaining judgment

I sat with this a bit trying to figure out what felt confusing. I think it's
that the word "judgment" is doing two different jobs in the same lecture — in
the Full Fact section it's the thing we should never automate, but just before
it there's the study that shows people made worse judgments of accuracy when
adjusting an algorithm than the algorithm. I think we want to give them some
really clear frameworks for thinking about judgement around AI coding, so I'm
wondering if we can make this clearer.

What if we more explicitly frame judgment as something that shows up all the way
through a process, and at each point you ask whether the criteria can actually
be written down, whether the person has information the model doesn't, and
whether either of them can check the result? That seems to explain both the
Zillow example and the study, and I think it heads off students concluding that
the safe move is always to add an approval step — which is sort of the thing the
study says goes wrong?

Small related thing — Full Fact deciding "which claims are worth checking" feels
like editorial judgment they managed to encode successfully, rather than
filtering? That might be the most useful part of that example for our students.
I just want them to have to think through the concept of when we encode judgment
(by training a model/filter) and when we reserve it for humans.
