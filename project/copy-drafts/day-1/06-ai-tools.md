# AI Tools

> New tools launch every week, and most of them will be irrelevant in a year. What doesn't go stale is knowing how to size one up fast.

## 1. Why This Matters

There's an AI tool for just about every task, and the list changes constantly. Memorizing today's lineup is a losing game — a comparison chart from six months ago is already out of date. What's actually useful is a repeatable way to size up whatever's in front of you: is this worth my time, and how do I get comfortable with it quickly?

You'll find a living list of tools worth knowing about on the [Resources page](/resources.html) — that's the place to check what's current, not this session.

## 2. Evaluating a New Tool

<!-- Content-architecture note: bare question list below, no sub-heading — flagged pattern from content-architecture-notes.md. -->

- Does it solve a real problem I have — not just "this is cool"?
- What's the learning curve?
- What's the cost — free tier, subscription, usage-based?
- Is there a free tier or trial to try before committing?
- Do I have the data it needs?
- Is it actually better than what I'm already doing?

> Default rule: stick with tools you know until you have a clear reason to add a new one.

## 3. Familiarizing Yourself With a New Tool

Once a tool clears the bar above, here's how to get a working sense of it fast — without losing a whole afternoon:

- **Start with a task you already understand.** Don't learn a new tool and a new problem at the same time — bring it something you could already do the slow way, so you can judge whether it actually helped.
- **Skim the tool's own quickstart before you improvise.** Five minutes of the real documentation usually beats twenty minutes of guessing.
- **Use a real example from your own work, not a toy one.** Toy examples make every tool look good. Your actual, messy data is the real test.
- **Note what surprised you — good or bad.** That's usually the most useful thing to remember about a tool, more than any feature list.
- **Get comfortable with 2–3 core capabilities before judging the rest.** You don't need to master a tool to know whether it's worth keeping around — you need to know what it's actually good at.

> The fastest way to know if a tool is worth learning is to bring it a real problem, not a demo.
