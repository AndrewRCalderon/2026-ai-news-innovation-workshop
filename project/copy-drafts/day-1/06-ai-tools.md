# Evaluating AI Tools

> New tools launch every week, and some will be obsolete just as quickly. What doesn't go stale is knowing how to size one up fast.

## 1. Why This Matters

There's an AI tool for just about every task, and the list changes constantly. Memorizing today's lineup is not an enduring skill. What's actually useful is a repeatable way to size up whatever's in front of you to determine whether it's the right fit for your problem, and how to get comfortable with it quickly?

You'll find a list of tools we believe are worth knowing about on the [Resources page](/resources.html).

## 2. Evaluating a New Tool

<!-- Content-architecture note: bare question list below, no sub-heading — flagged pattern from content-architecture-notes.md. -->

- Does it solve a real problem I have, not just "this is cool"?
- What's the learning curve?
- What's the cost (subscription, usage-based)?
- Is there a free tier or trial to try before committing?
- Do I have the data it needs?
- Is it actually better than what I'm already doing?
- Who are the creators, and have they made anything that I use or am familiar with?

> Advice: stick with tools you know until you have a clear reason to add a new one.

## 3. Familiarizing Yourself With a New Tool

Once a tool clears the bar above, here's how to get a working sense of it fast:

- **Start with a task you already understand.** Don't learn a new tool and a new problem at the same time. Bring it something you could already do the slow way, so you can judge whether it actually helped.
- **Skim the tool's own quickstart before you improvise.** Five minutes of the documentation and use cases usually beats twenty minutes of guessing. YouTube is your friend.
- **Use real data from your own work, or a realistic mock set.** Toy examples make every tool look good. Your actual, messy data is the real test.
- **Note what surprised you.** That's usually informative. The good, the bad and the ugly.
- **Get comfortable with 2–3 core capabilities before judging the rest.** You don't need to master a tool to know whether it's worth keeping around. Identify the tool's value proposition and test it. You are assessing what the creator says it's good at.
