# The eight source archetypes

These come from taking apart two hand-built local-news source lists (Houston, 46 feeds;
New York, 66) and asking what categories they fell into. The claim — tested on housing,
city politics and business, in three different cities — is that the same eight hold for
**any** beat. Only the institutions behind them change.

Use them two ways:

1. To **name candidates** for a place and beat you don't know well.
2. As a **checklist against a real story**: which of these did the reporter draw on,
   which did they ignore, and does one exist here for the ones they missed? The gaps
   are usually the most useful part of the answer.

---

## 1. Mandated publishers

**Bodies legally required to publish what they do** — agendas, minutes, notices,
filings, dockets, calendars.

The highest-value category. Publication is a legal duty rather than a communications
choice, so it is reliable, it is not spun, and it carries news *before* anyone reports
it.

- **Business**: city and county government feeds, SEC filings, WARN layoff notices
- **Civic / political**: council and committee agendas, election boards, court dockets,
  zoning and planning commissions, school board agendas
- **Look for**: anything with a statutory publication requirement, meeting-notice laws,
  or a public docket

## 2. Oversight & enforcement

**Bodies that inspect, license, audit, discipline or sanction.**

Enforcement actions are stories by construction: somebody did something wrong and an
institution wrote it down, with a date and a name.

- **Business**: bank regulators, contractor and trade licensing boards, workplace
  safety citations
- **Civic / political**: inspectors general, police oversight boards, ethics
  commissions, health and housing inspectors, campaign-finance enforcement
- **Look for**: who can fine, suspend, revoke or investigate on this beat

## 3. Structured open data

**Machine-readable registries** — permits, licences, inspections, contracts,
complaints, filings, property records.

The highest signal of the eight, and almost nobody watches them daily. Diffing a
registry surfaces things no press release announces: a new licence at an address, a
permit for a building nobody has reported on, a contract awarded quietly.

- **Business**: property appraisal rolls, building permits, deeds, new business licences
- **Civic / political**: 311 complaints, eviction filings, campaign finance, restaurant
  and building inspections, police stops, benefit-programme data
- **Look for**: a city or county open-data portal (Socrata, CKAN, ArcGIS), or any
  searchable public register. Worth extra effort — this is where original stories live.

## 4. Institutional newsrooms

**Large local institutions that publish their own announcements.**

They announce before they are covered, and almost nobody monitors them systematically.

- **Business**: universities, hospitals, ports, airports, utilities
- **Civic / political**: school districts, transit agencies, housing authorities,
  public health departments, public libraries
- **Look for**: the biggest employers and service providers in the place

## 5. Organized interests

**Groups organised around a stake** — unions, trade bodies, advocacy organisations,
community groups.

They surface conflict early, and they say out loud what institutions phrase carefully.

Cover **both sides** of a beat's central conflict. On housing that means tenant
organisations *and* landlord or building-owner associations; a list with only one is
a list that will miss half the story.

- **Business**: chambers of commerce, industry associations
- **Civic / political**: unions, tenant organisations, immigrant-rights groups, mutual
  aid networks, neighbourhood associations, good-government groups

## 6. Wires & PR, geo-scoped

**Press-release wires filtered to the place.**

Fast and noisy. Worth having, worth ranking low, worth never mistaking for a primary
record.

- Business Wire, PR Newswire, GlobeNewswire, filtered by place terms
- Nonprofits and agencies push releases through the same wires, so this is not a
  business-only category — only the query terms change

## 7. Emergency & alerts

**Real-time public-safety and disruption feeds.**

Disruption is news on every beat: a storm is simultaneously a business story, a housing
story and a public-health story.

- Weather services, power outage maps, boil-water notices, public-health advisories,
  emergency declarations, transit service alerts

## 8. Entity watchlists

**Specific named entities tracked individually** rather than by category.

A beat is usually a small number of recurring actors. Watching them by name catches
what a category filter misses.

- **Business**: locally headquartered public companies, big private employers
- **Civic / political**: named officials, agencies under scrutiny, large landlords,
  contractors holding public money, institutions with a record of problems
- **Look for**: who keeps appearing in the last year of coverage on this beat

---

## Using these well

**Two or three archetypes will dominate any given beat**, and that is fine — but check
the empty ones deliberately before concluding nothing fits. An empty "structured open
data" row usually means the portal hasn't been found yet, not that the city has none.

**Name the specific body, not the category.** "Cook County Clerk of the Circuit Court"
can be looked up, verified and subscribed to. "Court records" cannot.

**Archetype boundaries are fuzzy and it doesn't matter much.** A housing authority is
arguably an institutional newsroom and a mandated publisher. Pick one and move on — the
categories exist to prompt coverage, not to be argued about.
