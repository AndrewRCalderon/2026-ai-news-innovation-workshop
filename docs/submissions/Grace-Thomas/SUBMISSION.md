- **Hypothesis or problem statement:** On a breaking story, the request for comment is the step that gets rushed or skipped. A reporter working a deadline has to find each company's press contact, verify it's current, write an individually tailored email, and keep a record of who was asked and when — repeated once per company, in the same hour the story is being written. My hypothesis is that the research and drafting can be automated without automating the judgment, and that keeping a human on the Send button is what makes that safe.

- **What you're building:** RFC Bot — a Claude skill that turns a story brief into one individually written request for comment per company. It resolves each press contact and cites the page it read, labels how confident it is in that address, writes a short email tied to the specific news each recipient is being asked about, and keeps a tracking log for the "did not respond by press time" line. It never sends anything; the reporter reviews every draft and presses Send.

- **Solution:** Working end to end on three unrelated stories — a pink-cleats World Cup piece (Nike, adidas, New Balance, Puma), an NBC News story on Kalshi's flight-cancellation prediction contracts (Kalshi, Polymarket), and an Apple product launch. Two ways in: fill in `BRIEF.md`, or write in Google Docs and let the add-on hand the draft over — its sidebar asks one question, the deadline, and has one button. Either way you get one-click pre-filled Gmail drafts, matching `.eml` files, a single read-through file, and a tracking log. Emails are four sentences: who you are, what you're requesting comment on, what statement you need, and the deadline — no thesis paragraph, because on breaking news the company already knows what it announced. Contact research escalates through three tiers (raw HTML, text fetch, then a real browser) because the first two silently fail on `mailto:` links and bot-blocked sites. Companies reachable only by a media contact form still get a full draft plus a link to the form. Format rules are enforced by warnings that never block a draft, since a reporter on deadline shouldn't be stopped by a style check.

- **What Day 2 changed:** Day 2 was mostly finding out the Day 1 build didn't work. Contact research had discarded `mailto:` links and given up on sites that block automated requests — which had already saved a **wrong adidas address** behind a "MEDIUM" confidence label, meaning that email would have bounced. The emails opened by briefing companies on their own announcements. And everything had only ever run on the one story it was built alongside; the second story broke it within minutes, exposing a subject-header bug the first story was too short to trigger. All of it is fixed, and the project now has `requirements.md`, `architecture.md`, and `tasks.md` as its source of truth so sessions start from the spec instead of from an explanation. Then it moved into Google Docs: an add-on reads the story draft out of the Doc, and drafts land in Gmail as real drafts instead of compose links. Sending stays impossible by construction — the add-on runs in test mode, a commit hook refuses any change that adds a way to send, and an address the bot could not read off the company's own page is marked in the draft subject so it cannot be sent looking normal.

- **Known limits:** Everything routes through Gmail; Outlook users get `.eml` files, which is worse. The bot does not verify the story's premise — whatever goes in the brief gets put to a comms team in writing, so the reporting has to stand on its own first. The follow-up feature is paused on purpose rather than unfinished: this is a day-of tool, and a chaser that lands the next morning has nothing to chase. And it is not yet available in every Google Doc — an unpublished add-on works in one document at a time, and publishing is blocked by the Gmail permission the sidebar currently needs to write the drafts.
