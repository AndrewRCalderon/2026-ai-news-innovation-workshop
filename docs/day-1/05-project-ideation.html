01

The Design Process Is Circular

The mistake people make: thinking there's a straight line from "idea" to "finished project." Reality: design is messy and circular.

  1. 1

    Identify a problem

    Or think you do.

  2. 2

    Ask questions

    Understand it better.

  3. 3

    Form a hypothesis

    About how to solve it.

  4. 4

    Test / prototype

    Try something concrete.

  5. 5

    Learn something

    Whatever the result tells you.

  6. 6

    Reframe the problem

    Go back and revise your understanding.

  7. 7

    Repeat

    This is the whole cycle, again.

02

Finding a Problem Worth Solving

A series of questions meant to help you think, not to be "answered correctly."

Start here

  • What frustrates you about how journalism works (or could work)?
  • What task do you do repeatedly that feels inefficient?
  • What question do you want to be able to answer faster?
  • Where do you see people struggling — sources, readers, yourself, other reporters?

Go deeper

  • Who experiences this problem — you, reporters, editors, readers, multiple groups?
  • How do they currently solve it?
  • Why isn't the current solution good enough — cost, time, quality, accessibility?
  • If this problem disappeared, what would change?

Think about scale

  • How many people face this problem, and how often?
  • What's the cost of not solving it — time, money, missed stories, bad decisions?

Get specific

  • Can you describe the problem in one sentence? If not, you might not understand it well enough yet.
  • Is this a real problem, or are you assuming — have you actually talked to people about it?
03

Form a Hypothesis

A hypothesis is: "If I do X, then Y will happen." For an AI project: "If I use AI to [specific task], then [specific outcome] will happen for [specific person/group]."

  • "If I use AI to analyze court documents, then reporters can identify patterns 10x faster, so we can cover more cases with the same team."
  • "If I use AI to generate drafts of routine earnings reports, then business reporters can spend more time on investigative stories."
  • "If I use AI to tag and categorize reader comments by topic, then we can respond to common questions faster and improve engagement."

To sharpen it: What specifically will AI do — analyze, generate, summarize, categorize? What will change as a result? How will you know if it worked? Who benefits? And it has to be testable — can you actually try it, measure the outcome, and learn something either way?

04

Articulate Your Goal

Your goal is what you're actually trying to build and test. Three parts:

The problem
Clear, one sentence. "Reporters spend hours manually sifting through court documents to find relevant cases."
The solution
What you'll actually build. "Use AI to automatically categorize and flag court documents by relevance."
The success metric
How you'll know it worked. "Reporters find relevant cases 5x faster, and AI correctly flags 80%+ of relevant documents."
"I want to solve [PROBLEM] by building [SOLUTION] so that [OUTCOME]. I'll know it works when [METRIC]."
05

Reality Check

Before you settle on a goal, ask yourself the hard questions:

  • Is this actually an AI problem, or could a simpler tool solve it better?
  • Do you have access to the data AI would need?
  • Have you talked to people who experience this problem, or are you guessing?
  • Is this solvable in 3 days, or are you aiming too big?
  • Why hasn't someone else already built this?
06

Case Studies

Three real examples of this process in action.

Earnings reports
Business reporters spent 40% of their time on templated earnings reports. Built a tool that drafts them from structured financial data — 5 minutes per report instead of 30, freeing time for real investigation.
Coverage audit
A newsroom suspected uneven neighborhood coverage but had no data. Built a script that extracted locations from past stories and mapped them — revealed they covered low-income areas more than expected, but missed specific ethnic communities.
Fact-checking assistant
Reporters lose the thread with a source while stopping to verify a claim. Attempted a real-time assistant to flag issues during interviews — harder than expected; still in progress, needs careful prompt engineering so suggestions are trustworthy enough to act on.
07

Workshop Activity

Pick a problem you see in journalism, your own reporting process, or how information flows. Use the questions above to really understand it, then fill in the template.

🎯 Exercise: Build Your Problem Statement

Time: 30 minutes

Description: Fill in: Problem (one sentence) → Hypothesis (if I do X, then Y will happen for Z) → Goal (I want to solve [problem] by [solution] so that [outcome]) → Success metric (I'll know it works when...) → Why this matters.

Deliverable: A filled-in problem statement, hypothesis, and goal — not a perfect answer, just something concrete to build on tomorrow.

08

Key Takeaways

  • Your problem statement will evolve — that's good, it means you're learning.
  • Talk to people. Don't solve problems for people you haven't talked to.
  • Think small. Better to solve one small problem well than a huge one poorly.
  • AI is not always the answer. Be honest about whether it's the right tool.
  • Your hypothesis is a guess. It's okay to be wrong — the goal is to test and learn.
