01

What Everyone's Talking About

You can't understand AI's role in journalism without understanding what people actually disagree about. Some newsrooms are all-in; others are skeptical. Some technologists think we're building AGI; others think we're overhyped and about to plateau. Some ethicists say AI will amplify inequality; others say it's a democratizing tool.

💬 Before You Look

Prompt: Where do you think people actually disagree about AI's role in journalism? Call some out — we'll put your list on the board first.

Here's the list that tends to come up in this conversation:

  • Progress vs. caution — move fast and experiment, or slow down and get it right?
  • Accessibility vs. concentration — is AI democratizing, or does it concentrate power in whoever can afford the best models?
  • Efficiency vs. job displacement — more productive journalists, or fewer journalism jobs?
  • Accuracy vs. speed — can we report faster without sacrificing truth?
  • Transparency vs. trade secrets — how much should companies reveal about how their models work?
02

The Scaling Debate

One specific, narrower fight worth knowing on its own: will today's approach — more data, more compute — keep producing better models, or is it running out of room?

Optimists

  • Models keep getting better with more compute and data.
  • We're nowhere near the ceiling yet.

Skeptics

  • Returns are diminishing for the effort involved.
  • We may need a fundamentally different approach.

Nobody knows yet — both camps have people who know what they're talking about. Two other live debates worth knowing: the alignment problem (as models get more powerful, how do we make sure they do what we actually want?), and open source vs. proprietary — Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google release commercially with access controls; Meta and others release weights anyone can run locally. More openness means more experimentation but more risk of misuse; more control means less democratic access.

The clearest labor disruption so far isn't journalism — it's coding, where AI-assisted work is already reshaping what engineers spend their day on. Work is changing, not disappearing. That's the pattern most likely to repeat across journalism and most other knowledge jobs, too.
03

The Bigger Picture: Skeptics vs. Optimists

A wider, more general split than the scaling question above — this time about AI's overall impact. Both sides make real points. Worth holding both at once.

Skeptics Say

  • On accuracy: AI confidently makes things up — you can't publish without verification, which erases the speed advantage.
  • On labor: displacement is already happening in customer service, coding, and creative work.
  • On power: only huge companies can afford to train frontier models — that's concentration, not democratization.
  • On environment: training and running these models is genuinely energy-intensive.
  • On bias: models are trained on human text, so human biases — racism, sexism, class bias — come along for the ride.

Optimists Say

  • On accessibility: a solo freelancer can now access research and writing tools once limited to big institutions.
  • On creativity: a brainstorming partner that surfaces angles you wouldn't have thought of — amplifying, not replacing.
  • On efficiency: automating routine work (earnings reports, weather) frees people for deeper reporting, if it actually works that way.
  • On knowledge access: AI can explain a Supreme Court ruling or a physics paper in plain English.
04

Where Newsrooms Actually Stand

Most publications are in a "cautious experimentation" phase, but it looks different depending on size*:

Small / regional
Limited budget; mostly free tools for brainstorming and research.
Mid-size
Experimenting with data analysis and routine content, under strict disclosure and human-review policies.
Large
Dedicated AI teams, proprietary tools, public ethics conversations, explicit newsroom guidelines.

Common policies emerging across newsrooms:

  • Disclose when AI was used in reporting.
  • AI is a research tool, not a replacement for reporting.
  • Every AI-assisted piece is reviewed by a human before publication.
  • AI augments the team; it doesn't replace jobs.
  • Be transparent about AI's limitations — it can get things wrong.

Curious what real policies look like? Browse a compiled index of published AI policies from newsrooms worldwide.

Most newsrooms don't have a full answer yet. They're figuring it out as they go.
05

The Journalism Conversation

What newsrooms are actually experimenting with:

Washington Post
Haystacker, an in-house AI tool, helps reporters sift large datasets — video, photo, or text — for newsworthy patterns; stories are still reported and written by humans.*
Reuters
Fact Genie scans incoming press releases and financial documents and flags newsworthy alerts within 6–8 seconds of landing — journalists review and decide whether to publish. AI is also built directly into "Leon," the CMS every Reuters journalist writes in daily: headline suggestions, bullet-point summaries, error detection, style-guide prompts, all in the same window as the story.*
New York Times
Cautious by policy: staff can't use AI to draft or substantially revise articles, and any AI-generated images or video must be clearly labeled.*

What's actually happening: AI is used for data analysis, research, and drafting routine, templated stories. No major newsroom uses it to replace investigative reporting. However, newsrooms are using AI to augment investigative capacity. The tension is efficiency gains versus maintaining journalistic integrity.

  • When do I disclose that I used AI in my reporting?
  • Can I trust AI's analysis of a dataset, or do I need to verify everything myself?
  • If I use AI to brainstorm story ideas, is that still "real" journalism?

Many journalists fear AI will be used to replace them; publishers like the efficiency argument. The honest answer is it depends entirely on how it's used.

06

What's Actually at Stake

For journalists

Finding truth, interviewing sources, telling stories — that skill is more valuable, not less. What changes is how fast the routine research gets done. Learning to use AI well is becoming a job requirement, like learning to use Google was.

For news organizations

Can they get more efficient without sacrificing quality or trust — and stay competitive with organizations moving faster?

For the public

Will journalism survive and thrive, or get gutted by efficiency-obsessed publishers? Can readers trust the news if they don't know where AI played a role?

For you

The question isn't "will AI replace me?" It's "will I know how to use it?" There's a real opportunity to be among the people who figure out responsible, effective AI use in journalism early.
