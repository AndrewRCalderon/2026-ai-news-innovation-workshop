State of AI

We're at the invention phase, not the mature phase — and the rules are still being written.

10:00 AM·15 MIN·Lecture
01

The Moment

AI itself isn't new — machine learning has quietly shaped things like fraud detection and recommendation engines for over a decade. What changed is who gets to use it. Large language models turned something that used to require real technical training into something you open in a browser tab. That's worth taking seriously, and worth being skeptical about at the same time — the tools are real, and so is the hype around them.

  • AI went from "expert-only" to "something most people have at least tried."
  • We're at the invention phase, not the mature phase. Things are changing every week.
  • The rules, best practices, and ethics are still being written.

This mirrors other major shifts in how fast a technology went from novelty to normal:

1995Internet access
2007Smartphones
2010Social platforms
2022ChatGPT launch

ChatGPT launched with a free version, and within days AI went from a specialist topic to something millions of people were actually trying.

1M Users in 5 days*

One of the fastest consumer product launches ever — and it left one question hanging over everything since: what can I actually do with this?

02

A Cursory Timeline of Recent History

The path here wasn't sudden. It's the accumulation of decades of technical milestones and academic research — some at universities, some at big tech companies — that finally broke into public view.

  1. 2012

    Deep learning renaissance

    A neural network called AlexNet wins a major image-recognition contest by a landmark margin, using far more data and computing power than anyone had tried before.* Almost no one outside the field notices at the time — worth sitting with, since it's usually true: the shifts that turn out to matter most rarely look big while they're happening.

  2. 2016

    AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol

    Google's AI defeats the world Go champion — far more complex than chess. Mainstream news for the first time, but still entirely specialized.

  3. 2018–2020

    BERT, GPT-2, GPT-3

    Language models get genuinely good at understanding and generating text. GPT-3 introduces few-shot learning — the model picks up a new task from just a few examples shown in the prompt, without retraining.* Still gated behind research access.

  4. Nov 2022

    ChatGPT launches

    Free, open to anyone with an internet connection. Millions playing with AI within days. Newsrooms begin experimenting: brainstorming, drafting, analyzing data.

  5. 2023–2024

    The "Copilot" era

    AI built into everything. The debate shifts from "is this real?" to "how do we use this responsibly?" First lawsuits over training data land — The New York Times sues OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023, alleging its articles were used to train ChatGPT without permission.*

  6. 2024–2025

    Agents get hands

    Claude gains the ability to move a cursor, click, and type like a person.* OpenAI's Operator becomes the first agent product to break into mainstream tech coverage.* "AI acts on your behalf" stops being theoretical.

  7. 2025–2026

    Skills, then Cowork

    Anthropic launches Agent Skills, reusable packages of instructions an AI can load on demand.* Cowork brings that same autonomous work into a normal desktop app — no coding background required.*

  8. 2026

    The current moment

    AI embedded in newsrooms with explicit policies. Tone shifts from "AI is coming" to "how do we do this well." Frenetic integration work is underway — people are throwing things at the wall to see what sticks.

03

State of the Art

It's worth separating the hype from what's real.

Changed

  • Speed of deployment — prototyping now takes minutes, not months.*
  • Accessibility — no ML background needed, and adoption shows it: about half of U.S. adults now use AI chatbots, up from roughly a quarter in 2023.*
  • Capability scale — reasoning, coding, document analysis, all far beyond what these models could do three years ago.*
  • Cost — cheap enough for students and small newsrooms to experiment.*
  • Public conversation — no longer niche: 95% of U.S. adults have heard of AI, and those who've heard "a lot" nearly doubled in three years.*
  • Privacy tooling — enterprise AI plans now offer training opt-outs and data-retention controls, even if newsroom-grade guarantees are still maturing.*

Caveat

  • AI still hallucinates — confidently wrong, sometimes.*
  • It isn't "thinking" like humans; it's pattern-matching at scale.*
  • It can amplify biases already in its training data.*
  • Best on well-defined tasks, less so open-ended creative work.*
  • Privacy and data security remain genuinely thorny.*
  • We don't fully understand why these models work this well.*
04

Major Builders & Trends

Who's Building It

Tech giants
OpenAI (ChatGPT, GPT-4), Google (Gemini), Meta (Llama), Anthropic (Claude).
Specialized
Hugging Face, Midjourney (images), 11Labs (audio).
Open-source
Anyone can download and run some models locally.

"Best" depends on the task — Claude is strong at reasoning and writing, others excel at images or code, and open-source is closing the gap faster than most people expect. The names will change; the pattern — new capability, then months to figure out what to do with it — probably won't.

Who's Using It in Journalism

A different layer — newsrooms building on the tools above, not building foundation models themselves.

Large newsrooms
The New York Times' internal "Echo"* tool, The Washington Post's "Ember"* writing coach.
Small newsrooms
Sahan Journal*, Outlier Media* — same tools, very different scale.
For journalism

Efficiency gains, new storytelling possibilities, and genuinely hard ethical questions, all at once.

For society

Labor disruption, misinformation risk, the environmental cost of training, and whose voices get amplified.

For you

Understanding this technology is turning into a basic job skill, the same way Google Docs, Slack, and spreadsheets did.

05

What This Means for Journalists

AI is a tool in your kit now, the way Google Docs or Slack is.

You Can

  • Draft initial story outlines.
  • Analyze large datasets you feed it.
  • Brainstorm headlines or angles.
  • Explain complex topics so you understand them better.
  • Fact-check claims — imperfectly.
  • Generate ideas for visual presentation.

You Should Not

  • Replace reporting — it synthesizes, it doesn't originate.
  • Publish AI text unvetted — it's confidently wrong, often.
  • Use it without disclosure.
  • Assume it's objective — trained on biased human text.
Not "will AI replace me?" but "what does this let me try that I couldn't before?"
