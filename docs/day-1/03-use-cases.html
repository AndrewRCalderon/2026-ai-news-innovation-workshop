01

Use Cases in Journalism

See all 9 examples
Data journalism
The NYT's internal tool, Cheatsheet, analyzed 10,000 names from a Puerto Rico tax-incentive registry and rated each for investigative interest stemming from a reporter's "I can't Google 10,000 people, but a machine can." It later processed 500 hours of leaked Zoom audio (~5M words), turning an unmanageable dataset into a story before Election Day. Takeaway: you still verify everything yourself.*
Coverage auditing
THE CITY asked ChatGPT to map four years of stories by NYC neighborhood to confirm they covered underserved areas more than they realized, while surfacing real gaps. Takeaway: AI misreads location and context.*
Headline ideation
Feed a draft or topic to Claude, ask for ten angles, pick the strongest. Hearst's regional dailies built a Slack bot doing exactly this at scale, with human review required before anything ships. Takeaway: AI headlines skew clichéd — it's a brainstorming partner, not a final product.*
Accessibility
"Explain this so a high schooler would understand," then iterate. A two-person team at Persian-language newsroom Zamaneh Media used this approach to cut translation time from days to under an hour. Takeaway: guarantees clarity, not accuracy. You still need subject-matter expertise to fact-check.*
Local government monitoring
iTromsø, a 20-person Norwegian newsroom, built an AI tool that scans 12,000+ municipal documents a month and flags newsworthy ones — cutting daily document research from hours to about 10 minutes, now used across 35 papers. Takeaway: still needs a reporter's judgment on what's actually a story.*
Automated claim-spotting
Argentine fact-checker Chequeado's Chequeabot scans dozens of outlets and speeches in real time to flag checkable claims. By the organization's own account, roughly half of certain fact-checks now start there, a share that's grown since the tool launched in 2016 as it's expanded to cover podcasts and dozens of channels. Takeaway: it flags candidates. A human still has to verify each one, and Chequeado's own AI-use policy requires that review.*
Automated local content
Swedish publisher MittMedia uses automation to turn structured data into thousands of local real-estate and sports stories a year that reporters couldn't otherwise cover — 61,800 articles in one year at one network. Takeaway: works when the underlying data is clean and structured.*
Records & FOIA analysis
DocumentCloud's free AI add-ons let any newsroom, no coding required, summarize and search large public-records dumps. Takeaway: built for small newsrooms without engineering staff, so it's only as good as the documents you feed it.*
No-code automation
Probably the most common entry point at small newsrooms, even if it's the least flashy: Simon Galperin (also profiled in People to Follow) ran an earlier local-news project on Zapier alone, indexing 200+ local sources into one feed, auto-drafting posts from press releases, and pushing them to a newsletter and social. In his words, "This news product wouldn't exist in its present form without Zapier." Takeaway: it's automation, not intelligence. It still needs someone to design the workflow and catch when a source changes format.*
02

Use Cases Outside Journalism

A few examples from outside of journalism that might spark your imagination:

See all 11 examples
Customer service
A chatbot handles "why was this retracted?" and "how do I subscribe?" so people can focus on real complaints. This is exactly what handles subscriber support at most newsrooms, not just other industries. Takeaway: confidently wrong answers need human oversight.*
Content moderation
Flags spam and off-topic comments for human review at scale, the same tooling most newsroom comment sections run on. Takeaway: tested against matched sentence pairs, toxicity classifiers score African American English as more toxic than equivalent Standard English in up to 77% of cases. That's a measured bias, not a hypothetical one.*
Search & recommendation
Understands intent, not just keywords; suggests relevant follow-up reading. Takeaway: can create filter bubbles if not designed carefully.*
Video & audio
Transcribes interviews, extracts quotes, generates captions automatically. Takeaway: accuracy drops with accent and dialect. A 2024 study of real healthcare conversations found the best-performing transcription system had a 50% word-error rate for Black speakers versus 33% for white speakers. Proofread transcripts.*
Sentiment analysis
Flags where anger or concern concentrates across thousands of comments. Takeaway: misreads sarcasm and irony — "great job on that terrible decision" can read as positive.*
Scientific research
Google DeepMind's AlphaFold predicts a protein's 3D structure from its sequence, solving a 50-year "grand challenge" in biology. Takeaway: predictions are computational hypotheses that still need lab verification.*
Legal
Harvey AI now supports 142,000+ legal professionals across 1,500+ organizations, including roughly half of the Am Law 100 — used for contract review and, closest to journalism's own work, discovery: parsing massive document dumps for what actually matters. One firm, Lynn Pinker Hurst & Schwegmann, says it saves 8+ hours per lawyer per week using it for early case assessment across hundreds of files. Takeaway: still requires a lawyer to verify what it surfaces — same as any AI-assisted document review.*
Finance
Mastercard's Decision Intelligence Pro scans roughly a trillion data points to flag fraudulent transactions in under 50 milliseconds, improving detection rates 20% on average — up to 300% in some cases. Takeaway: a high false-positive rate means real transactions get blocked too; it's a filter, not a final judge.*
Healthcare
IDx-DR became the first FDA-authorized autonomous AI diagnostic tool, screening for diabetic eye disease without a specialist reading the image. Takeaway: works only within a narrow, validated task — it defers ambiguous cases rather than guessing.*
Software & coding
A 2023 study of real, accepted Copilot suggestions in live GitHub projects found 27.3%, roughly 1 in 4, contained a security weakness. Takeaway: AI coding assistants still reproduce flaws present in their training data, even as the rate improves.*
Accessibility (outside journalism)
Be My Eyes' "Be My AI," powered by GPT-4 with vision, describes photographed scenes for blind and low-vision users. Takeaway: documented hallucinations — confidently describing objects or text that aren't actually there.*
03

Not a Refined Use Case (Yet)

  • Originating quotes or facts. A reporter at a Wyoming paper resigned in 2024 after admitting an AI tool had fabricated quotes attributed to the state's governor.* A senior reporter at Ars Technica, a publication that covers AI for a living, was fired in 2026 for the same failure: fabricated quotes attributed to a real engineer.* If specialists who understand these tools that well can still be caught by their own fabrications, treat anything AI hands you as a fact or a quote as unverified until you've confirmed it independently.
  • Investigative reporting. AI can't call a source, dig through an archive with judgment, earn a whistleblower's trust, or read between the lines of what someone isn't saying, the parts that actually make it investigative. It can help with pieces of the process (see Data Journalism and Records & FOIA Analysis above); it just can't do the whole thing.
  • Original breaking news. It synthesizes existing information, and struggles to produce real-time information but it's getting close.
  • Opinion requiring deep expertise. AI can draft structure, but not credibility or experiential perspective.
  • Stories requiring human experience. Homelessness, racial justice: the reporting itself is human work.
  • Real-time verification. Training data has a cutoff, but that's a workflow problem, not a hard wall. Point it at live search or a real-time feed and it can work with current information.
04

The Emergent Patterns

One large evaluation found current AI agents succeed on nearly 100% of short, well-defined tasks, but under 10% of tasks that take a human more than four hours. Task length and ambiguity predict failure better than anything else does.*

Works When

  • The task repeats itself: The task is repetitive and structured.
  • The input data already checks out: Input data is high-quality.
  • You're panning for gold: It's finding needles in haystacks — pattern recognition, lead generation, surfacing what a human would miss at scale.
  • Good enough beats exhaustive: You need an "at least this many" count, not an exhaustive one — finding as many examples as you can, not every single one.
  • A human still signs off: It augments human work, not replaces it.
  • You'd catch the error: You can verify the output yourself.
  • A wrong guess costs little: Mistakes are tolerable.

Doesn't Work When

  • Nobody's called yet: The task needs original reporting or investigation.
  • It doesn't know what it doesn't know: Models default to a confident-sounding guess instead of admitting uncertainty, so accuracy breaks down exactly where you can least afford it.
  • The full picture won't fit in view: Every model has a hard limit on how much text it can hold in context at once, and correlating enough sources or a long enough archive runs past it.
  • What's unsaid matters most: Context and nuance are everything.
  • A byline is a promise: Readers need to trust a human made the call.
  • You had to be there: It requires real-world experience you haven't had.
05

The Practical Questions

Questions to Ask Before Reaching for AI

  • Does this need original judgment every single time — or can I design the process so judgment only happens on a smaller, surfaced set?*
  • Is my input data trustworthy and high-quality?*
  • Can I actually verify the output myself before it goes out?*
  • Would a mistake here be tolerable, or costly to my credibility?*
  • Am I augmenting my own work, or trying to replace judgment I should be exercising myself?*
  • Does this depend on real-time information, or a relationship/trust only a human can provide?
