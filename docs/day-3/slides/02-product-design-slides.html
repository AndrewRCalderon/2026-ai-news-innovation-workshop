Product Design

Not AI for AI's sake, but whether its use earns trust.

10:30 AM·45 MIN·Lecture
01

The Confident-Wrongness Problem

Eight AI search tools tested on 1,600 news queries: over 60% factually wrong.

ChatGPT misidentified 134 articles but flagged low confidence only 15 times out of 200. The failure mode isn't "the tool doesn't know," it's sounding equally confident whether it's right or wrong.

Don't assume demand either: 49% of one survey's respondents said they had zero interest in AI chatbots for news.

02

Framing Changes Everything

The same process and product, described two different ways, can get different trust measures.

"Human-led, AI-assisted" got 43% comfort, versus 21% for "AI-led, with human oversight," describing the same workflow. Same work, different label, double the comfort.

And it's a moving target: chatbot use for news rose from 7% to 10% globally in a year, and people mostly reach for one to act on the news, not to feel something about it.

03

The One Pattern Every Case Study Agrees On

Answer only from what you actually own and trust, and say so when you can't.

Chowbot (SF Chronicle)
Only ~1,000 vetted reviews, never general web knowledge.
Ask FT
Restricted to FT's own archive, footnoted citations.
Adelaide (Forbes)
"We only want to provide our users with our trusted journalism."
Rai (Rappler)
Hyper-strict guardrails against outside information.

Scope is the key. Use cases that stick to a narrow, well-defined job answer reliably. A large corpus or a general-purpose tool increases the likelihood of falsehood and inaccuracy.

04

Design for Transparency

Telling people you used AI is an interface decision, and the details of that decision measurably change trust.

Detailed disclosures actually made readers slightly less trusting than a one-line label, even though most preferred the detailed version. Researchers call this the "transparency dilemma."

One study made this concrete: 93% of an AI article's claims linked back to their source, versus 25% for human-written comparisons, and readers rated it more transparent. Six patterns for giving readers that choice:

  • Highlight-for-glancing: mark the passages AI touched, scannable without breaking flow.
  • Info button: a small icon readers click for full disclosure, not a forced block of text.
  • Outlet-level disclosure: publish your AI policy once, not per article.
  • Proportional AI-ratio visualization: a percentage bar showing how much AI touched.
  • Visual trust stamp: a badge for responsible AI use.
  • "No AI used" label: disclosure as a signal that works both directions.
