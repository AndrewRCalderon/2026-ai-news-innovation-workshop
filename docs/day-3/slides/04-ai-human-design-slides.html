AI-Human Design

Neither human-in-the-loop nor full automation is the safe default. Both are design decisions.

12:15 PM·45 MIN·Lecture
01

Human in the Loop Isn't the Default

Neither is full automation. Sometimes you add a human step you don't strictly need, because they benefit from sitting with the output. Other times their perspective isn't needed at all, so you automate top to bottom and check only the final result.

  • How cheap is it to notice and undo the mistake?
  • Does the tool tell you when it's unsure?
  • Can you see why it did what it did?
02

A Checkpoint Isn't Automatically Safer

554 people given an algorithm's prediction to adjust. It made things worse.

46.5% higher false-positive rate than the algorithm alone

It also introduced racial bias that wasn't in the algorithm itself. Adding a human checkpoint has to be tested, not assumed safe.

03

What a Real Checkpoint Looks Like

Full Fact draws an explicit line: automate the filtering, never the judgment call.

Fully automated
Classification, extraction, ranking, matching against past fact-checks.
Never automated
Deciding if a claim is true, writing the fact-check itself.

A real test for review: read it, understand it, validate it, then write your response in your own words. Rewriting is the only step that proves the rest happened.

04

Two Failures, Opposite Directions

Zillow removed a working checkpoint under growth pressure, purchase volume doubled, then a $421M quarterly loss followed and the business shut down.

Section 02's study found adding a checkpoint can backfire too. Same lesson twice: it's a specific, testable choice, not a value applied uniformly.

05

Same Judgment, When You're Coding

Full Fact's filtering and Zillow's checkpoint are the same decision you make constantly while you build: does this step need a human?

A formatting fix is cheap to undo, let it commit. A change to how your app handles credentials is expensive to undo, read it line by line. Plan Mode is this same choice, built in: a checkpoint you add on purpose before something costly, so you catch a wrong assumption while it's still cheap to fix.
