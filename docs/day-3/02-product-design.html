Product Design

01

The Confident-Wrongness Problem

A study testing eight AI search tools on 1,600 news queries found more than 60% of answers were factually wrong.* That's not the dangerous part on its own. The dangerous part: ChatGPT misidentified 134 articles, but flagged low confidence only 15 times out of 200 answers.* The failure mode isn't "the tool doesn't know." It's the tool sounding exactly as confident when it's wrong as when it's right.

Whatever you build today needs to design around that, not assume it away. And don't assume demand either: in one survey, nearly half of respondents said they had zero interest in AI chatbots for getting news.* Build for the person who'll actually use it, not the assumption that they will.

02

Framing Changes Everything

Here's the single most useful finding for anyone designing an AI feature: the same underlying process, described two different ways, gets wildly different trust.

43% comfort: "human-led, AI-assisted"

Versus 21% comfort for "AI-led, with human oversight," describing functionally the same workflow.* Same work, same amount of human review, different label, double the comfort. This isn't spin, it's a real design lever: how you describe what your tool does changes whether people trust it, independent of what it actually does.

One more number worth knowing: only 33% of people believe journalists actually check AI output before publishing, and that belief tracks closely with trust.* Visible verification, not claimed verification, is what moves that number.

03

The One Pattern Every Case Study Agrees On

Across every real newsroom AI product this research turned up, one design choice shows up again and again: answer only from what you actually own and trust, and say so when you can't.

SF Chronicle's Chowbot
Answers draw only from ~1,000 vetted restaurant reviews, never general web knowledge. Outside that scope, it says so instead of guessing.*
Financial Times' Ask FT
Restricted to FT's own archive, footnoted citations, a stated time range for sources. It still misidentified an ex-candidate as active, a stale-knowledge gap worth planning for explicitly.*
Forbes' Adelaide
Chief Digital Officer: "We only want to provide our users with our trusted journalism." Launched to 5% of readers first, expanded once the pattern held.*
Rappler's Rai
Built with "hyper-strict guardrails to prevent outside information from seeping into results," designed to say "I don't know" rather than fabricate.*

The clearest evidence for why scope matters comes from a study of four small-newsroom bots. The two that stuck to a narrow, unchanging job (subscription FAQs, local election basics) answered reliably. The two that tried to search an entire, growing story archive couldn't keep the underlying data current with a 3-person newsroom, and one was pulled after readers asked for its removal.* Scoping tightly isn't a limitation you settle for, it's the actual reason the reliable ones work.

04

Design the Disclosure

Telling people you used AI isn't a compliance checkbox, it's an interface decision, and the details of that decision measurably change trust.

Detailed disclosures actually made readers slightly less trusting than a simple one-line label, in one controlled study, even though most readers said they preferred the detailed version anyway.* The fix isn't picking one over the other, it's giving people the choice:

  • Detail-on-demand: a small info icon that reveals full disclosure on click, instead of forcing a block of text on every reader.
  • Highlight-for-glancing: a visual marker on the specific passages AI touched, scannable without breaking reading flow.
  • Outlet-level transparency: publish your AI policy once, don't repeat a full disclosure on every single piece.

These three patterns come directly from reader research, not designer intuition.*

🎯 Apply This

Time: A few minutes, individually.

Description: For your own project: what's the one source of truth you'll scope your AI to, and what will it say when a question falls outside that? Then draft one sentence of disclosure using this template: "We used [tool] to help us [what it did]. We [verified/reviewed] it by [how]."*

Deliverable: One sentence you could actually put on your project today.
