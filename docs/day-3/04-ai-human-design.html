AI-Human Design

Neither human-in-the-loop nor full automation is the safe default. Both are design decisions, made stage by stage, and either one can be gotten wrong.

01

Human in the Loop Isn't the Default

AI is often used to automate a process end to end. For a while, the default answer to a safety question was to add a human checkpoint. Human oversight matters, but it isn't automatically the responsible choice any more than automation is. The real question is which one actually fits a given stage of a given process.

Two situations that look similar from the outside can call for opposite answers. Sometimes you slow a process down by adding a human step you don't strictly need. You do it anyway because the person benefits from sitting with the output. It deepens their own understanding of the work, or gives them context they'll need at a later stage. Other times a human's perspective, context, or verification genuinely isn't needed at a given point. You let the process run automated from top to bottom and bring a person in only at the very end, to check the final result.

Microsoft's own guidelines for human-AI interaction, tested across 20 shipped products, don't give a hard threshold for which situation you're in. They give three things worth checking before you let a tool act unsupervised.*

  • How cheap is it to notice and undo the mistake? If correction is cheap, more autonomy is defensible. If it's expensive, the checkpoint needs to come before the action, not after.*
  • Does the tool tell you when it's unsure, or does it guess with the same confidence every time?*
  • Can you actually see why it did what it did, or is it a black box you'd have to take on faith?*

Separately, Google's own design guidance names four situations to keep a human checkpoint regardless of how good the AI is: the task has real physical, emotional, or financial stakes; it's something the person enjoys doing; there's a social obligation to be present for it; or it depends on idiosyncratic taste that's hard to actually specify.*

02

A Checkpoint Isn't Automatically Safer

Here's the finding worth sitting with: a controlled study gave 554 people an algorithm's prediction and let them adjust it, the textbook "human in the loop" design. It made things worse.

46.5% higher false-positive rate than the algorithm alone

Only 23.7% of participants actually beat the algorithm by adjusting it; 64.1% did worse.* Worse still: it introduced a racial bias that wasn't in the algorithm by itself. When the score suggested higher risk than a person's own instinct, that push toward a higher score was 25.9% stronger for Black defendants than white defendants.*

"How can we expect people to navigate the balance between their own judgment and a risk assessment's when they are unable to accurately assess their own or the algorithm's performance in the first place?"*

The lesson isn't "don't use humans." It's that adding a human checkpoint is a design decision that has to be tested, the same as any other, not assumed safe because it sounds responsible.

03

What a Real Checkpoint Looks Like

Full Fact, a 15-journalist fact-checking newsroom, draws an explicit line: automate the high-volume filtering, never automate the judgment call.

Fully automated
Topic classification, claim extraction, ranking which claims are worth checking, matching against past fact-checks.*
Never automated
Deciding if a claim is actually true, and writing the published fact-check. "We never ask it 'Is this claim true?' because no model can reliably answer that."*

AP's own newsroom rule states the same idea plainly: any AI output is "unvetted source material," staff still apply normal sourcing standards before it goes anywhere near publication.*

One practical test for whether a "review" step is real or theater, worth stealing directly: read it, understand it, validate it, then write your response in your own words. That last step is the only one that actually proves the first three happened, since claiming to have reviewed something costs nothing, but rewriting it yourself does.*

04

Two Failures, Opposite Directions

One company removed a checkpoint that was working. A study found adding one can backfire. Both are real.

Zillow Offers originally had pricing experts vet every algorithm-generated home value estimate. Under growth pressure in 2021, "Project Ketchup" explicitly barred those experts from adjusting the algorithm's numbers. Purchase volume doubled the next quarter. Then the algorithm's blind spots, no longer caught by anyone, caught up: a $421 million loss in a single quarter, the business shut down, about 25% of the company laid off.*

Set that against Section 02: giving people an algorithm's output to adjust made outcomes worse, not better, in a separate controlled study. Put together, the two aren't a contradiction, they're the same lesson twice. Whether you keep the human or remove them, it's a specific, testable design choice for this exact task, not a value you apply uniformly everywhere.

05

Same Judgment, When You're the One Coding

Full Fact deciding which claims are worth checking, and Zillow's removed pricing checkpoint, are versions of the same decision you make constantly while you build. Does this particular step need a human, or can it run on its own?

A formatting fix Claude proposes is cheap to undo and easy to verify at a glance. Let it commit. A change to how your app handles user data or credentials is expensive to undo and hard to verify just by reading a summary. That one is worth reviewing line by line before it ships, even if it slows you down. Plan Mode, from Day 2, is built around this same choice. It's a checkpoint you add on purpose before a costly action, not because you don't trust the tool, but because reviewing the plan is where you catch a wrong assumption while it's still cheap to fix.

Sometimes the right call is to read every line anyway, even on a low-stakes change, because doing so is how you actually learn what your own codebase does. That's the same tradeoff from Section 01. Not every checkpoint exists to catch an error. Some exist because you're the one who benefits from being in the loop.

🎯 Apply This

Time: A few minutes, individually.

Description: For one part of your project: is it currently automated, checkpointed, or fully manual? Using the questions from Section 01, is that actually the right call for this stage, or did you default to it without deciding?

Deliverable: One sentence stating the decision and why.
